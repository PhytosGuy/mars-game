The challenge

NASA’s 2026 Space Mission Design Game challenge (difficulty: Beginner/Youth and Intermediate, subject: Games) asks teams to:

“…create an interactive game that allows participants to design, manage, and simulate a complete space mission while making engineering decisions, managing limited resources, and evaluating how each choice shapes the success of their mission.”

Designing a mission means balancing the science goal, the spacecraft, its instruments, the rocket, the orbit, power, mass, communications, budget and lifetime. A choice that helps one part often hurts another: a better instrument brings more science, but also more mass, power, data and cost. Students rarely get to try a whole mission from concept to operation, so these trade-offs stay abstract. NASA asked for a game that closes that gap.

What the brief asks for

  • Define a mission objective and its requirements
  • Design a spacecraft and choose its scientific instruments
  • Choose a launch vehicle and an orbit or trajectory
  • Manage mass, power, budget, data, communications and time
  • Show how each decision affects the rest of the mission
  • Simulate the mission and explain why it succeeds or fails
  • Single-player or a team of up to four
  • Accessible to every level, with simple explanations

NASA’s resources for the challenge, which we used: Design and Develop Science Missions, the General Mission Analysis Tool (GMAT), JPL’s Concept Maturity Levels, Goddard’s Mission Design Lab, NASA Mission Viz, the Webb telescope learning game, CubeSat 101, the 2023 challenge Visualize a Space Mission Using Virtual Reality, and the Canadian Space Agency’s games and activities.

2

Our objective

Our game Mars: Last Colony uses captivating and educational gameplay to teach users how to plan and structure a NASA space mission, how to land a shuttle on Mars, and how to sustain life on Mars. Throughout the game, players are met with challenges and decisions that impact the future of their colony.

Educational

Real NASA data, NASA’s own mission design process and 34 mission facts, each delivered at the moment it matters.

Fun

A landing you fly yourself, a colony to run, a planet to explore and up to four friends to play with. When learning is this engaging, players stay long enough for it to stick.

Stage 1 · Mission Design Lab

The lab is its own page. It follows the same steps NASA uses to grow an idea into a mission, measured by JPL’s Concept Maturity Levels and the study stages of Goddard’s Mission Design Lab:

  1. 1
    StartThe four mission segments: launch, space, ground and user. Pick a difficulty: one, two or three science objectives.
  2. 2
    CML 1 · Office HoursChoose what to discover: Mars’s ancient water, its lost atmosphere, signs of ancient life, marsquakes, a relay link, the first galaxies, exoplanet air or Earth’s climate.
  3. 3
    CML 2–3 · Concept Development and Trade StudyCompare a 3U or 6U CubeSat, an orbiter, a lander, a rover or a space telescope by mass, payload, power, cost and risk.
  4. 4
    CML 4 · Point DesignFit instruments that see the right kind of light, within the payload limit; choose solar or nuclear (RTG) power and, for telescopes, the mirror size.
  5. 5
    Mission segmentsPick a rocket that can lift your mass to your destination: Electron, Falcon 9, Atlas V 541, Ariane 5 or SLS. See the ground network and who uses the data.
  6. 6
    TrajectoryFind the launch window and watch your flight path across a 3D solar system, then scrub through the mission timeline (with an optional VR view).
  7. 7
    VerifyCheck every requirement, as NASA does with vibration and thermal-vacuum tests.
  8. 8
    LaunchName your mission and watch it launch. It is scored, compared with real NASA missions and saved.

Every choice in 3D. Each objective, spacecraft, instrument, power system, mirror and rocket has its own 3D model. Your spacecraft is assembled live as you add instruments, then shown inside the rocket’s fairing, and the rocket lifts off when you launch.

Every mission saved. Launched missions are kept in My missions with their model, score, mass and cost. You can reopen a report or edit a copy, and each mission gives your colony research points in the game.

Stage 2 · Landing

Five landing sites

Before you land you choose where. Each site is inspired by a real place on Mars, and each one looks and plays differently from the moment you step out.

The landing is flown from a detailed cockpit with a big window and two live screens: a LIDAR terrain scan and a chase camera with flight statistics.

  1. 1
    OrbitSwing in towards Mars, 6,779 km across, red from iron oxide dust.
  2. 2
    Atmospheric entryAbout 20,000 km/h; the heat shield reaches around 1,300 °C. The landing flies itself, because radio takes 5 to 20 minutes each way.
  3. 3
    ParachuteA supersonic parachute opens about 11 km up (Perseverance’s was 21.5 m wide).
  4. 4
    Heat shield releasedThe LIDAR sees the ground; Terrain-Relative Navigation steers to a safe spot.
  5. 5
    Powered descentYou take the throttle. Mars gravity is 3.71 m/s²; touch down slower than 3 m/s.
  6. 6
    Walk outTurn round, open the hatch and walk down the stairs onto Mars.

Stage 3 · Sustain

Life support is a daily balance

3.4 kgoxygen a crew of four uses per sol
16 Lwater per sol
7.2 kgfood per sol
43%of Earth’s sunlight reaches Mars
  • Oxygen Unit +6 kg O₂ per sol, scaled from NASA’s MOXIE experiment
  • Ice Drill +39 L water per sol, only where ice lies buried
  • Greenhouse +5 kg food per sol, but it draws 7 L of water
  • Power solar arrays give 2.5 kW at noon and nothing at night; batteries hold 30 kWh; a fission unit gives 10 kW day and night. In a shortfall the habitat and oxygen come first.
  • 17 structures built from mined ore and regolith

People, failures and progress

  • The crew an Engineer (30% fewer faults), a Botanist (15% more food), a Geologist (25% more research) and a Medical Specialist, with a Robotics Specialist and a Planetary Scientist arriving later
  • Things fail habitat seals leak oxygen, water lines leak, drills jam, grow lights burn out: every repair is a race against your reserves
  • Research 12 projects in 7 branches, such as Improved Electrolysis (+80% oxygen), Water Recovery (half the water, as on the ISS) and an Electrostatic Dust Shield (60% less dust)

Six missions to self-sufficiency

  1. 1TouchdownPower and oxygen online
  2. 2First SurvivalLocate ice, grow food
  3. 3Secrets BeneathLava tubes and organics, verified twice
  4. 4Beyond the HorizonLaboratory and field science
  5. 5A Growing WorldA landing pad and new crew
  6. 6Humanity’s New HomeFission power and a full sol in surplus

The ice biome

Every site has an ice biome: a frozen plain with mountains of blue ice crystal that you can climb. The water mission sends you there to scan an ice signature, just as NASA’s orbiters map buried ice for future crews. On the Ice-rich Plains the whole landing site is one.

A planet that pushes back

Dust storms darken the sky and smother solar arrays. Meteor strikes leave craters but expose fresh ore. Marsquakes open fissures and can seal cave entrances. Five landing sites are modelled on Jezero, Gale, Utopia, Tharsis and Elysium, with lava-tube caves to explore by rover and on foot. Pip, a small robot companion, fetches items, helps you drill and warns you about danger, and up to four friends can play together.

Learning in context

The game holds 34 NASA facts, each appearing when it is most relevant, so players learn by living the situation:

  • During a dust storm a planet-wide storm in 2018 ended Opportunity’s 15-year mission
  • While producing oxygen MOXIE generated 122 g of oxygen from the Martian atmosphere
  • As the ground shakes InSight recorded over 1,300 marsquakes, the largest near magnitude 4.7
  • Under the night sky Phobos orbits so fast that it rises in the west and sets in the east

Discoveries become technology. Scan a bright crust and learn it is perchlorate, first identified by Phoenix in 2008. That knowledge unlocks scrubbers that raise greenhouse yields by 15%: one of 11 discoveries drawn from real rover and orbiter findings.

Against the judging criteria

1Impact

Free in any modern browser on desktop or phone, with nothing to install, so any classroom can play. It turns abstract mission trade-offs into decisions players feel.

2Creativity

Mission design, a piloted landing and a living colony in one journey: you live with the consequences of the mission you planned.

3Validity

Live and playable today. Launch windows, transfer times and Δv come from Kepler’s laws and JPL data; colony numbers are scaled from MOXIE, Kilopower and the ISS.

4Relevance

Built around the Space Mission Design Game brief and NASA’s own resources, with real NASA data at the core rather than added on.

5Presentation

Every image here is captured in the game, and the whole experience is one link away.

How it was made

Made with Claude Code

Team NAA designed the game and built it with Claude Code, Anthropic’s AI coding tool, as an AI pair programmer. We described each feature, tested it, and decided what went in, from the landing sequence to the 3D Mission Lab.

  • Three.js (WebGL) 3D graphics: procedural terrain, cel-shaded lighting, caves, weather, the cockpit screens and the Mission Lab models
  • Plain JavaScript about 7,700 lines across 43 scripts, with no build step and no framework
  • PeerJS · WebRTC co-op for up to four players, sent player to player, with the host keeping the world in sync
  • Supabase optional accounts, profiles and friends, protected by row-level security
  • NASA and JPL data planetary positions, fact sheets and published mission results

Plays on any device

  • Phones and tablets touch controls with a joystick and buttons; hints name the on-screen buttons, such as Interact, and text is sized to fit small screens
  • Graphics settings Auto, High, Medium and Low, or Custom: switch shaders, glow, colour grading, shadows and cel shading on or off, and set the resolution
  • Smooth start while the game loads, a “wait for the game to load” message appears instead of buttons that don’t work yet
